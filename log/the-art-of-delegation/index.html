The art of delegation is deciding what you will never see

I built a company of agents so I would stop being the bottleneck. Within four days I was the bottleneck again — this time for forty-four decision cards.

For the last few months I have been building an operating system for a small company in which most of the staff are AI agents. A CTO, a product lead, a head of marketing, a sales director, an architect whose only job is to say what could go wrong. Each of them has a role file, a scope, and a list of things it is allowed to do.

The point of all of it was one sentence I wrote to myself: I do not want to be the bottleneck. The business has to make money without me being welded to the computer.

So I delegated. And then I watched delegation fail in a way I did not expect.

Delegating work is easy. Delegating judgement is not.

Handing out tasks turned out to be the trivial part. The agents wrote code, drafted landing pages, prepared campaigns. What they could not do was decide whether any of it should go out — so every one of those decisions came back to me.

Between 29 October and 1 November the system opened forty-four decision cards. Approve this post. Pick option A, B or C for the landing page. Accept these names. Merge this change. Each card was reasonable on its own. Together they were a second job, and I was doing it on a phone between other things.

I had delegated the work and kept every decision. That is not delegation. That is a queue with my name at the end of it.

Three things that actually changed it

One voice. Agents were reporting to me directly, each in its own chat, each with its own format. I could not see one answer in the context of another. Now exactly one agent talks to me. Everyone else reports to that agent, which filters. If I write to someone else, my message is forwarded to it word for word. The rule I gave the system was blunt: I need one interface and one person talking to me, not ten.

Decisions in batches. Instead of one card per question, each project collects its open questions into a single card: numbered items, each with a recommendation. I can answer with one letter — accept all — or with the numbers I want to change. The cost of a decision is mostly the context switch, not the thinking. Batching pays the switch once.

A written mandate with tiers. This was the real change. On 31 October I signed off a charter for a board of agents with three levels:

  • L0 — the owner of an area just does it.
  • L1 — the board decides: the area owner, the risk architect and the chair, unanimously. I get a daily digest and a veto window.
  • L2 — only me. Strategy, pricing, contracts, partners, new channels, anything irreversible, and anything published under my name.

Marketing, for example, can publish on its own — but only on channels I have already approved and in a voice I have already approved, with twelve hours for me to object. A LinkedIn post in my name is still mine to sign.

What delegation actually is

Not a list of tasks you hand out. A list of decisions you agree, in advance, never to see — and a short, explicit list of the ones you will always see.

Why the list has to be written down

Most delegation I have seen runs on mood. On a good week things are let go; on a bad week everything is pulled back. People can read that. Agents cannot — and, honestly, people should not have to.

Writing the tiers down did something I did not anticipate: it forced me to say which mistakes I am willing to live with. An L1 decision will sometimes be wrong. That is the price. If I am not willing to pay it for a given category, the category belongs in L2, and I should stop pretending I have delegated it.

The other thing that has to be written down is the one decision I refuse to give away: nothing gets merged or deployed to production without my explicit go. Not because the agents are bad at it. Because that is the one place where being wrong is expensive and quiet at the same time.

The test I use now

When something lands on my desk, I ask one question before answering it: should this have reached me at all? If the answer is no, the answer to the card matters less than fixing the rule that sent it. One rule change removes a whole category of future cards. Answering the card removes one.

The art of delegation, as far as I can tell, is that boring. Decide what you will not see. Write it down. Accept the mistakes that come with it. And treat every decision that reaches you anyway as a bug in the mandate, not as work.

If you are running agents and still approving everything they do, you have built a very fast way to fill your own inbox. I work on the part that decides what never needs to reach you. Tell me what is landing on your desk.
